# CompTIA Security+ Notes: 5.1 Security Governance

This section covers the administrative side of cybersecurity: the rules, frameworks, and roles that dictate how an organization manages its security posture and complies with laws and regulations.

## 1. Policy Hierarchy

Governance documentation follows a strict hierarchy, moving from high-level management directives down to technical, step-by-step instructions.

* **Policies:** 
  * **Level:** Highest.
  * **Nature:** Mandatory.
  * **Description:** Broad, high-level statements of management's intent. They define the "what" and the "why" but not the "how." They are typically approved by C-level executives and rarely change.
  * *Example:* Acceptable Use Policy (AUP), Password Policy ("All users must use strong passwords").

* **Standards:**
  * **Level:** High/Medium.
  * **Nature:** Mandatory.
  * **Description:** Defines the specific, technical rules, metrics, or hardware/software required to satisfy the policy. 
  * *Example:* "Passwords must be exactly 14 characters long and require MFA" or "All laptops must use AES-256 bit encryption."

* **Procedures:**
  * **Level:** Low (Operational).
  * **Nature:** Mandatory.
  * **Description:** Detailed, step-by-step, chronological instructions on exactly "how" to implement the standard.
  * *Example:* A 10-step guide for IT support on how to configure BitLocker encryption on a new employee's laptop.

* **Guidelines:**
  * **Level:** Low.
  * **Nature:** **Non-mandatory (Optional).**
  * **Description:** Recommendations, suggestions, or best practices. They provide advice when there isn't a strict standard in place.
  * *Example:* "It is recommended to clear your browser cache weekly to improve computer performance."

## 2. Governance Frameworks

Frameworks provide a structured methodology for organizations to assess and build their security programs, rather than guessing what they should do.

* **NIST CSF (Cybersecurity Framework):**
  * Developed by the US National Institute of Standards and Technology. 
  * Primarily voluntary for the private sector but widely adopted globally.
  * Built on five core functions: **Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, and Recover.**

* **ISO/IEC 27001:**
  * An international standard published by the International Organization for Standardization.
  * Focuses on building, maintaining, and continually improving an **ISMS (Information Security Management System)**.
  * Unlike the NIST CSF, an organization can be officially audited and certified as ISO 27001 compliant, which is highly valuable for global business.

* **CIS (Center for Internet Security) Controls:**
  * Previously known as the SANS Top 20 (now the CIS 18).
  * A highly prescriptive, prioritized list of specific technical controls and best practices designed to stop the most common and dangerous cyber attacks. It tells organizations exactly what technical defenses to implement first.

## 3. Governance Roles

Understanding who is responsible for data is crucial for compliance and security auditing.

* **Data Owner:**
  * **Role:** Usually a senior executive or department head (e.g., VP of Sales owns customer data; HR Director owns employee data).
  * **Responsibility:** Bears ultimate, legal responsibility for the data. They determine the data's classification level and dictate who is allowed to access it.

* **Data Custodian:**
  * **Role:** Typically an IT administrator or security engineer.
  * **Responsibility:** They do not own the data; they protect it. They implement the technical controls (backups, encryption, access control lists) as mandated by the Data Owner.

* **Data Controller (Privacy / GDPR concept):**
  * **Role:** The organization that collects the data.
  * **Responsibility:** Determines the *purpose* and *means* of processing personal data. If your company collects user emails for a newsletter, your company is the Data Controller. They are legally liable for privacy breaches.

* **Data Processor (Privacy / GDPR concept):**
  * **Role:** A third-party vendor.
  * **Responsibility:** Processes data *on behalf* of the Data Controller. They do not own the data and cannot use it for their own purposes. 
  * *Example:* If a company (Controller) uses AWS (Processor) to host its database, or a payroll company like ADP (Processor) to pay its employees.
